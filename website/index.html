Introduction

Present-day Hindu culture is pivoted solely on the cow. Its material and spiritual concepts are both engulfed in cow worship. Such an animal worship is known as zoolatry. The Hindutva brigade propaganda machinery uses the politics of the cow to mobilize the blind Hindu masses and works them into a frenzy. The taboo on cow slaughter is one of the pillars of the Hindutva ideology. According to M.S. Golwalkar, a Hindutva ideologue, cow slaughter in India began with foreign domination: “The Muslims started it and the Britishers continued it” (Bunch of Thoughts, p. 496).

In the NCERT school textbook for Class VI (2002) we read:

“Among the animals the cow was given the most important and sacred place. Injuring or killing of cow was prohibited in the Vedic period. The cow was called Aghnya (is not to be killed or injured). Vedas prescribe punishment for injuring or killing cow by expulsion from the kingdom or by death penalty, as the case may be.” Social Sciences Textbook for Class VI, p. 89

But the theory that in Vedic times there was no cow slaughter is historically inaccurate. Although the cow was revered and treated as sacred, it was also offered as food to guests and persons of high status. The fact remains that ancient Hindu scriptures clearly permit the consumption of meat, even of cows. True scholars, and not modern frauds, know this. For example, Swami Vivekananda admitted:

“You will be astonished if I tell you that, according to the old ceremonials, he is not a good Hindu who does not eat beef. On certain occasions he must sacrifice a bull and eat it.” The Complete Works of Swami Vivekananda, Vol. 3, p. 536
“There was a time in this very India when, without eating beef, no Brahmin could remain a Brahmin.” Ibid., Vol. 3, p. 174

1. Yajña and Animal Sacrifice

यज्ञः

In Hinduism, Yajña is a ritual of sacrifice derived from the practice of Vedic times. How to deal with the animal that is to be sacrificed — whether goat, horse or cow — is mentioned in the Aitareya Brāhmaṇa of the Ṛgveda as follows:

“6. …Turn the animal’s feet northwards. Make its eyes go to the Sun, dismiss its breath to the wind, its life to the space, its hearing to the directions, its body to the earth. In this way the Hotar (priest) connects it with these worlds. Take off the entire skin without cutting it. Before opening the navel tear out the omentum. Stop its breathing within (by stopping its mouth). Thus the Hotar puts breath in the animals. Make of its breast a piece like an eagle, of its arms (two pieces like) two hatchets, of its forearms (two pieces like) two spikes, of its shoulders (two pieces like) two kāśyapas (tortoises), its loins should be unbroken (entire); make of its thighs (two pieces like) two shields, of the two kneepans (two pieces like) two oleander leaves; take out its twenty-six ribs according to their order; preserve every limb of it in its integrity. Thus he benefits all its limbs. Dig a ditch in the earth to hide its excrements.

7. Present the evil spirits with the blood.” Aitareya Brāhmaṇa, Book 2, §§6–7 (tr. Martin Haug)

Subsequently the same text instructs on the distribution of the different parts of the sacrificial animal among the priests (thirty-six named portions). The text concludes that those who divide the animal correctly are guided to heaven; those who do otherwise are “like scoundrels and miscreants who kill an animal merely.”

The Pūrva Mīmāṃsā Sūtras of Jaimini and Śabara’s commentary confirm the same procedure in detail (Sūtras 3/6/18, 3/7/28, 3/8/43). Pandit Yudhiṣṭhira Mīmāṃsaka, the modern authority on the school, states plainly that the division of the meat in the Aitareya Brāhmaṇa proves that animals were sacrificed and their meat consumed by the Brahmins, and that there is no strong evidence to regard these passages as later interpolations.

2. Refuting “There Is No Violence in Yajña”

अध्वरः

Modern apologists frequently cite Yāska’s Nirukta 2.7 — “Adhvara iti yajñanāma — dhvarati hiṃsākarma tatpratiṣedhaḥ” — to argue that adhvara means “non-violent” and therefore no animal could ever have been harmed. The tradition itself, however, explains what the word protects — and it is not the animal.

“For once when the gods were engaged in sacrificing, their rivals, the Asuras, wished to injure (dhvar) them; but, though desirous of injuring them, they were unable to injure them and were foiled: for this reason the sacrifice is called adhvara (‘not damaged, uninterrupted’).” Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa 1/4/1/40

Sāyaṇa on Ṛgveda 1/1/4 gives the same explanation: the sacrifice is called adhvara because it is protected by Agni and remains uninterrupted by Rākṣasas. Even the later tradition does not deny that the animal dies; it reinterprets the death as a promotion (Prabhupāda on Bhagavad-gītā 18/3; Manu Smṛti 5/39 and 5/44).

3. Animal Sacrifice in the Saṃhitās — Including the Cow

वेदाः

Chapter 24 of the Śukla Yajurveda contains a long enumeration of animals assigned to different deities. The Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa (13/2/2/1–10) confirms that these animals are actually to be immolated. The Ṛgveda itself describes Indra feasting on the result:

“Your worshippers express with the stone fast-flowing exhilarating Soma-juices for you. You drink them. They roast bulls for you, you eat them when you are invoked, Maghavan, to the sacrificial food.” Ṛgveda 10/28/3

Sāyaṇa’s commentary on the same verse and his introduction to Atharvaveda 9/4/1 speak without embarrassment of the Brahmin killing the bull and offering its meat to the deities.

4. The Aśvamedha Yajña

अश्वमेधः

Ṛgveda 1/162 follows the horse-sacrifice victim from the procession to the spit. The verses leave nothing to the imagination: the goat is brought first, the horse is bound, dissected, roasted, and its flesh is offered and eaten. Later polemical attempts to turn aśva into “nation” or medha into “intellect” are contradicted by the plain language of the hymn, by the Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa’s detailed ritual instructions, and by the Mahābhārata’s description of the same sacrifice.

5. Meat-Eating in the Vedas, including Cow Meat

The Sanskrit word for meat is māṃsam. Yāska’s Nirukta 4/3 derives it from “it is honoured” or “it is thought/enjoyed.” Durga’s commentary explains that it is prepared for a person who is honoured and is enjoyed with pleasure.

Atharvaveda 18/4/20 and 18/4/42 speak of dishes “rich in flesh.” The Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa 3/4/1/2 explicitly compares the guest-offering for Soma to cooking “a large ox or a large he-goat” for a king or a Brāhmaṇa.

6. Goghna — The Guest for Whom a Cow Is Killed

Pāṇini’s Aṣṭādhyāyī 3/4/73 treats goghna as an irregular formation in which the affix denotes the recipient. In ordinary usage it does not mean “killer of a cow” but “he on whose coming a cow is killed” — i.e., a distinguished guest. The same explanation is given by Bhaṭṭoji Dīkṣita in the Siddhānta Kaumudī and by Sāyaṇa in the Mādhavīya Dhātuvṛtti. Guests, priests, sons-in-law and returning Vedic students were therefore called goghna because a cow was slaughtered in their honour.

7. Refuting the “Aghnyā” Argument

The claim that the Vedas universally prohibit the killing of cows because they are called aghnyā (“not to be killed”) does not withstand scrutiny. The frequently quoted verses (Yajurveda 13/49, Ṛgveda 1/164/40, etc.) are prayers asking Agni or other deities not to harm one’s own productive dairy cows; they simultaneously ask that harm fall upon the forest cow or the enemy. They are not legal prohibitions covering every bovine. Classical commentators (including Sāyaṇa) often read the same passages metaphorically (cloud, rain, earth). The existence of the term aghnyā for certain cows does not cancel the ritual and hospitality texts that prescribe the slaughter of other cattle.

8. Other Evidences of Beef-Eating

The Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad 6/4/18 prescribes rice cooked with the meat of a young bull or ox for a couple desiring an intelligent son. Ādi Śaṅkara’s commentary accepts the plain meaning. Similar prescriptions appear in the Gṛhyasūtras for the madhuparka rite offered to distinguished guests.

9. Animal Sacrifices in the Mahābhārata

The epic contains numerous references to large-scale animal (including bovine) sacrifices performed by the Pāṇḍavas and other kings. The Aśvamedha performed by Yudhiṣṭhira is described in detail in the Aśvamedhika Parvan, including the cooking and offering of the limbs and marrow.

10. Testimony of Ancient Indian Medical Texts

Both the Caraka Saṃhitā and the Suśruta Saṃhitā discuss the properties of the flesh of various animals, including the cow and the bull, as articles of diet under specific conditions. These medical treatises treat bovine meat as a known and usable food, not as an absolute taboo.

11. Testimony of Classical Scholars

Beyond the passages already cited, the great medieval commentators — Sāyaṇa on the Vedas, Śabara on the Mīmāṃsā, and others — consistently explain the sacrificial and hospitality texts in their literal sense. Later apologetic reinterpretations that turn every animal into a vegetable or every act of killing into a pure metaphor appear only in modern reformist literature.

Conclusion

The cumulative evidence of the Brāhmaṇas, the ritual Sūtras, the Saṃhitās, the Upaniṣads, the epics, the medical literature, and the classical commentaries is unambiguous: animal sacrifice, including the sacrifice of cattle, was an established part of Vedic and early post-Vedic practice, and the flesh was distributed and consumed. The modern absolute taboo on beef is a later historical development, not the position of the oldest layers of the tradition.
